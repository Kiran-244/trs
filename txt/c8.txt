Questions, Stories, and Lessons Beyond the Syllabus

Some teachers teach lessons.

You taught conversations.

And perhaps that's why your classes never felt confined to grammar, vocabulary, or literature.

They often wandered into places that textbooks never could.

One afternoon, only a handful of us were in the classroom. Most of our classmates hadn't arrived yet. It was just me and two of my classmates sitting on the front bench—as we almost always did.

Instead of beginning the lecture immediately, you began telling us a story from your childhood.

I may not remember every detail exactly the way it happened, but I remember how it made me feel.

You told us about a time when your father decided to change your school. You and two of your friends went to the new school together, hoping for a fresh beginning.

I remember one small detail that somehow stayed with me all these years.

One of your friends was walking when she stumbled and fell. You mentioned that she had styled her hair in a puff that day, and the incident drew laughter from the students around her.

It was such a simple story.

Yet somehow, it felt so real.

Things didn't quite work out at that new school, and eventually you all returned to your old one. And when you did, people looked at you differently. There were playful remarks, little teasing comments—"Oh, so you went to the new school?"—the kind of things children often say without realizing how deeply they can stay with someone.

Looking back now, I realize it wasn't the story itself that stayed with me.

It was the way you told it.

You never shared stories to fill time.

You shared them because every story carried a lesson hidden between its lines.

Those moments reminded me that teachers are not just people who stand in front of a classroom. They, too, were once students with awkward moments, uncertainties, childhood memories, and dreams of their own.

It made you feel more human.

And perhaps that is one of the reasons I admired you so much.

Another conversation that has stayed with me began with a question.

A simple question.

"What is God?"

Everyone answered differently.

When my turn came, I replied,

"For me, God is hope. God is faith."

Coincidentally, we had just received our first-semester results that day. I was grateful for the SGPA I had earned, and perhaps that feeling of gratitude quietly shaped my answer.

Then you shared a deeply personal experience about the loss of a close family member. You spoke about grief, about the helplessness that follows such moments, and about how hope and faith become the only things we can sometimes hold on to when words are no longer enough.

It wasn't a discussion about religion.

It was a discussion about being human.

I have always been drawn to questions that don't have a single correct answer—questions about life, purpose, hope, existence, and what it means to be human. Maybe that personal inclination made me appreciate conversations like these even more.

But I don't think it was only that.

It was your curiosity.

Your openness.

The way you invited students to think instead of simply telling them what to think.

There was something deeply refreshing about a classroom where ideas mattered as much as answers.

Looking back, I realize that what drew me towards your classes wasn't merely the subject you taught.

It was the atmosphere you created.

You made learning feel like a conversation rather than a performance.

And somewhere along the way, without even realizing it, I began looking forward not just to English lectures, but to the thoughts, stories, and questions they would bring.
