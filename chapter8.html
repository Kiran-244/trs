CHAPTER VIII

The Student Within the Teacher

The people who teach us are often still learning themselves.

Scroll to Begin

Semester II • 2024

One thing I always admired about you was the way you spoke about your own teachers.

Most of us only knew you as our English professor.

But every now and then, through a story or a passing remark, we caught a glimpse of the student you once were.

I still remember you telling us about your Psychology teacher.

You didn't just describe what she taught.

You spoke about her presence.

Her personality.

The way she carried herself.

The admiration in your voice made it clear that some teachers leave an impression long after the classroom is gone.

At the time, I found that strangely beautiful.

Here was a teacher whom her own teacher had inspired.

It reminded me that learning doesn't end when someone begins teaching.

The best teachers continue to carry pieces of the people who once taught them.

You often shared little anecdotes during our lectures.

Sometimes they came from literature.

Sometimes from history.

And sometimes from Greek mythology.

I still remember listening to you tell us about Homer.

Not just as a name from ancient history, but as someone whose stories had survived for thousands of years.

You also spoke about Odysseus—his wit, his resilience, and the way he relied on intelligence rather than strength alone.

It was the first time Greek mythology felt alive to me.

Until then, it had simply been another unfamiliar term.

After that lecture, I found myself wanting to know more.

I started reading about Greek mythology on my own.

Years later, that curiosity was still with me.

I even remember texting you one day to ask if you had any recommendations for books on Greek mythology.

Looking back now, I don't think I was only asking for a book recommendation.

I was returning to the person who had sparked that curiosity in the first place.

That's something I've come to appreciate even more with time.

The influence of a teacher is rarely confined to the subject they teach.

Sometimes it appears years later—in the books we pick up, the questions we ask, or the interests we never expected to develop.

Your classes never made learning feel like something that ended with an examination.

They made it feel like the beginning of a lifelong conversation with ideas.

"The greatest teachers don't simply give us answers. They leave us with curiosities that quietly accompany us for years."

Perhaps that's why, even today, whenever I come across Greek mythology or think about inspiring teachers, I find myself remembering your classroom.
